# Sentence clarity reference

Use this reference to diagnose difficult prose and compare viable revisions. Apply the principles together; no single pattern determines the best sentence.

## 1. Match actors with subjects

Readers usually understand a clause faster when its grammatical subject names the person, thing, system, process, or idea that performs the main action.

Ask:

- Who or what changes, decides, causes, observes, requires, or produces something?
- Is that actor visible?
- Is the actor the subject, or is it hidden in a possessive phrase, prepositional phrase, passive construction, or omitted entirely?

Before:

> The research team's review of the samples led to the discovery of an error.

After:

> The research team reviewed the samples and discovered an error.

The revision makes the team the subject and gives its two actions to the verbs.

The actor does not need to be a person:

> The cache retains successful responses for ten minutes.

Here, *the cache* is a clear subject because it performs the relevant action.

## 2. Express important actions as verbs

A nominalization is a noun or noun phrase that expresses an action or quality, such as *analysis*, *decision*, *failure*, *movement*, or *comparison*. Nominalizations are useful, but a sentence becomes harder to process when they hide its main actions.

Common clues include:

- nouns ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ence*, *-al*, or *-ing*;
- weak verb phrases such as *perform an analysis*, *make a decision*, *conduct a review*, or *provide an explanation*;
- important actors hidden after *by*, *of*, or a possessive;
- clauses built around *there is*, *there are*, *it is*, *occur*, or *take place* when a specific action is available.

To revise:

1. Find the noun that carries the main action.
2. Recover its verb.
3. Identify who or what performs that action.
4. Make that actor the subject when it matches the sentence's focus.

Before:

> Our requirement is that the vendor perform a verification of every record.

After:

> We require the vendor to verify every record.

Before:

> There was a rapid increase in memory use after deployment.

After:

> Memory use increased rapidly after deployment.

Do not convert a nominalization merely because it has a recognizable suffix. Keep it when it names the topic under discussion or an established concept:

> The first migration failed. The second migration preserved all records.

Repeating *migration* keeps the topic stable and is clearer than forcing an actor-action rewrite.

## 3. Put the subject and verb early and together

Readers hold introductory and interrupting material in memory while waiting for the clause's grammatical core. Long delays make that core harder to find.

Revise when:

- a long introductory phrase appears before the main subject;
- an extended aside separates the subject from the verb;
- the sentence begins with an empty placeholder before introducing the real topic;
- a long subject contains several actions or qualifications.

Before:

> After several weeks of testing across three operating systems and two network configurations, the engineering team approved the release.

Possible revision:

> The engineering team approved the release after several weeks of testing across three operating systems and two network configurations.

Before:

> The engineering team, after several weeks of testing across three operating systems and two network configurations, approved the release.

Possible revision:

> After testing the release for several weeks across three operating systems and two network configurations, the engineering team approved it.

Choose the version that gives the intended emphasis and best connects to the preceding sentence. Moving all context to the end is not always best.

## 4. Put simplicity before complexity

Give readers the clause's structural core before asking them to process extensive qualifications. A short subject and specific verb create that core.

When a subject is long, try one of these moves:

- move secondary details after the verb;
- turn an embedded action into its own clause;
- split the sentence;
- replace repeated detail with a precise reference whose meaning is clear in context.

Before:

> The report's detailed account of the outage, its effect on customers, and the team's recovery work provides the evidence for the recommendation.

After:

> The report recommends the change based on evidence about the outage, its effect on customers, and the team's recovery work.

Alternative when the report itself does not make the recommendation:

> The report describes the outage, its effect on customers, and the team's recovery work. That evidence supports our recommendation.

The alternatives differ in agency. Select the one that preserves the facts.

## 5. Put familiar information before new information

Clear paragraphs usually connect each sentence to what the reader already knows before introducing the next point. The opening establishes the topic; the ending often carries the new or emphasized information.

Less connected:

> The service writes each event to the log. Operators investigate failed requests with a request identifier.

More connected:

> The service writes a request identifier with each event. Operators use that identifier to investigate failed requests.

The repeated idea creates a visible bridge between the sentences.

This principle can outweigh a preference for a more active or shorter sentence:

> The first migration failed. It was interrupted by a network timeout.

The passive second sentence keeps *the first migration* as the topic. If the timeout is the important new actor, write instead:

> The first migration failed. A network timeout interrupted it.

## 6. Use passive voice deliberately

Passive voice is useful when:

- the actor is unknown: *The records were deleted overnight.*
- the actor is irrelevant: *The samples were stored at 4°C.*
- the receiver is the established topic: *The request is validated before it enters the queue.*
- naming the actor would distract from the result or procedure.

Revise the passive when it hides responsibility that readers need:

> The access policy was changed without review.

If the actor is known and relevant:

> The administrator changed the access policy without review.

If the actor is unknown, do not guess.

## 7. Split overloaded sentences

Consider splitting a sentence when it contains:

- more than one main actor with unrelated actions;
- several causes, contrasts, or conditions;
- a long qualification that obscures the main claim;
- a pronoun with more than one plausible antecedent;
- coordination that makes the logical relationship ambiguous.

Keep the original relationship explicit after splitting. Words such as *because*, *although*, *therefore*, *if*, and *unless* often carry essential logic.

Before:

> The job retried after the server closed the connection, but the token had expired, which caused authentication to fail and the scheduler to stop the run.

After:

> The job retried after the server closed the connection. However, the token had expired, so authentication failed and the scheduler stopped the run.

The revision retains the sequence and causal links rather than turning the sentence into disconnected statements.

## 8. Diagnose without overcorrecting

Use the following questions during a final pass:

1. Can a reader identify the main actor and action in each clause?
2. Does the subject express the intended topic?
3. Does the main verb carry the important action or state?
4. Are the subject and verb easy to find and close enough to connect?
5. Does each sentence begin from information the reader can recognize?
6. Does the sentence end on the information that should receive emphasis?
7. Would splitting the sentence clarify its logic?
8. Did the revision preserve every condition, qualification, relationship, and uncertainty?
9. Did any revision assign responsibility or causation that the source did not establish?
10. Does the result sound natural for the audience and genre?

If the answer to the last three questions is uncertain, prefer the faithful version and flag the ambiguity instead of silently resolving it.
