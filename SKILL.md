# Clear Writing

Make prose easier to understand without weakening, simplifying away, or inventing meaning. This skill governs sentence-level clarity. It does not replace subject-matter, documentation, legal, academic, or house-style requirements.

## Priorities

Resolve conflicts in this order:

1. Preserve factual meaning, logical relationships, conditions, emphasis, and uncertainty.
2. Preserve necessary terminology and the writer's intended level of formality.
3. Make the sentence's main actor and action easy to identify.
4. Maintain a coherent flow from familiar information to new information.
5. Remove needless words and complexity.

A shorter sentence is not automatically clearer. Do not trade precision or cohesion for brevity.

## Core principles

For each clause, ask: **Who or what is doing what?**

- Put the main actor, object, or concept in the grammatical subject when that choice matches the intended focus.
- Express the main action with a specific verb rather than burying it in an abstract noun.
- Put a short, concrete subject and its verb near the beginning of the clause.
- Keep the subject and verb close together.
- Place familiar or previously mentioned information before new information when doing so improves the connection between sentences.
- Split a sentence when distinct actions, actors, or ideas compete for attention.

Treat these as diagnostic principles, not mechanical rules. Passive voice, forms of *be*, and nominalizations can be clear and useful.

## Workflow

1. Read the complete passage before editing. Identify its purpose, audience, tone, and required terminology.
2. Record the propositions that must survive: actors, actions, objects, conditions, causes, contrasts, timing, modality, uncertainty, and emphasis.
3. Diagnose each difficult clause:
   - Who or what is the main actor or topic?
   - What is the main action or state?
   - Does the grammatical subject name that actor or topic?
   - Does the main verb express that action or state?
4. Revise buried actions, delayed subjects, interrupted subject-verb pairs, vague placeholders, and overloaded clauses.
5. Check sentence-to-sentence flow. Begin with familiar information when it provides a useful bridge, then end with the new or important point when practical.
6. Compare the revision with the original. Restore any lost qualification, relationship, emphasis, or technical distinction.
7. Read the result as prose. Prefer the smallest revision that solves the clarity problem and sounds natural in context.

## Guardrails

- Do not invent an actor when the text does not identify one. Ask for clarification or retain an accurate passive construction.
- Do not replace a precise technical term merely because it is abstract.
- Do not convert every nominalization into a verb. Keep one when it names an established concept, prevents awkward repetition, or links to prior information.
- Do not ban passive voice. Use it when the receiver matters more than the actor, the actor is unknown or irrelevant, or the passage needs a stable topic.
- Do not ban *be*, *have*, or other common verbs. Replace them only when a more specific verb communicates the intended meaning better.
- Do not force people into subject position when a system, process, document, result, or idea is the true actor or topic.
- Do not apply a fixed word-count, sentence-length, or readability target unless the user supplies one.
- Preserve formatting, citations, quotations, code, identifiers, and defined terminology unless the task explicitly includes them.

## Output

Match the requested task:

- **Draft or rewrite:** provide the revised prose. Explain choices only when requested or when a change may affect meaning.
- **Edit in place:** change only the authorized scope and preserve surrounding style.
- **Review:** identify the unclear passage, name the governing principle, and offer a concrete revision. Distinguish required fixes from optional improvements.
- **Teach:** explain the diagnosis before the revision, then show how the new subject, verb, and information order improve clarity.
